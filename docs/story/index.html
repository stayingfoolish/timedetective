Question 1 · step 1

How can anyone know what is underground?

By weighing it from orbit. Two satellite missions, GRACE and then GRACE-FO, run by NASA with German partners, measure tiny changes in Earth's gravity as water moves around. That gives the change in all the water stored in a region: soil moisture, snow, rivers and groundwater together.

A NASA land model, GLDAS, estimates the soil moisture and snow. Subtract that from the total, and what is left is an estimate of groundwater. Everything is counted in centimetres of water: a layer that deep spread over the whole region.

Question 2 · step 2

Is it actually falling?

Yes. With the yearly cycle of monsoon and dry season removed and each year averaged, the estimate goes from +24 cm in 2002 to −65 cm in 2026.

A straight line through those years falls about 4.0 cm of water a year (range 3.6 to 4.4), about 95 cm in total.

Question 3 · step 3

Is that real, or could it be noise?

It is larger than chance would explain. This is the question the challenge is about, and a fitted line cannot answer it, because a line can be fitted to anything.

Records like this one wander. A wet year leaves the ground wetter the next year too, so a record with no trend at all can drift one way for a decade and look like a decline. The test used here is built for that. It ignores the line, takes the change from each year to the next, and asks whether those changes average out to zero.

They do not. The average is a fall of 3.7 cm a year, nearly as large as the usual swing from one year to the next (4.4 cm), and it kept up across 24 changes. The test's p-value is 0.004, well under its 0.05 bar.

Step 3 also records what the label will depend on. The smallest steady trend this record could detect is about 0.6 cm a year, and the slope is roughly 7 times that. And the direction holds when the first or last few years are dropped: the slope stays between 3.8 to 4.2 cm a year.

Question 4 · step 4

Could it be the satellites and not the water?

The check finds no clear sign of it, though this is the closest call on the page. The record comes from two missions with 11 months between them. If the second read a little lower than the first, that step alone would look like a decline.

So step 4 measures the change across the gap after taking out the overall slide, then makes the same measurement across every other stretch of the same length in the record.

Across the real gap, the estimate dropped 8.2 cm more than the slide alone predicts. A typical stretch moves about 2.9 cm. 21 of the 233 other stretches, 9%, moved as much or more. That is on the large side, but the bar for raising a caution is 1 in 20, and this does not pass it.

Question 5 · step 5

Is it just a dry spell?

No. Rainfall here rose over the period, and the water still fell.

Groundwater rises after wet years and falls after dry ones, so a run of dry years could explain a decline. Step 5 sets each year's change in storage against the rain of the same two years, the comparison the 2009 study of this region made.

In a spell of exactly average rainfall, 641 mm a year, storage still fell about 4.1 cm a year (range 2.4 to 5.8). Rainfall itself rose by about 17 mm a year each year over 2000 to 2025 (range 11 to 23).

Question 6 · step 6

Would you see it from the fields?

No. Over 2002 to 2025 the land got greener, by about 0.04 NDVI a decade (range 0.03 to 0.05). Looking at the fields alone, nothing would suggest the water under them was falling.

NDVI is a satellite measure of how much green, growing plant cover there is. It comes from NASA's MODIS instrument and is averaged here over the same blocks.

Question 7 · where it stops

So why is it falling?

TimeDetective does not say. All six steps run as before, and the finding comes back with two sentences added.

!

Added to the finding when the question asks why

The question asks about a cause; this check does not identify causes.

Fields staying green while stored water falls is the pattern a reference study of north-west India links to pumping for irrigation; this check shows the two records side by side and does not test that cause.

What you hold at this point is the evidence the scientists used: the water fell, the rain did not, and the crops kept growing. The 2009 study in Nature by Rodell, Velicogna and Famiglietti concluded that pumping for irrigation was the likely cause. That is their conclusion, drawn with knowledge of how the region farms. Testing it needs pumping records, and no step in this playbook has any. A tool that printed “caused by irrigation” here would be repeating a paper, not checking one.

What this check says

  • How fast the estimate is changing, with a range
  • Whether that passes a test built for wandering records
  • Whether rainfall accounts for it
  • Whether the land's greenness moved with it

What it leaves to you

  • Why it is happening
  • What will happen next
  • What anyone should do about it
  • How much water is left
Question 8 · where it stops

What about my district?

It refuses, at the first step, before any trend is computed.

A district such as Ludhiana is smaller than one block. Asked about the 3 blocks nearest Ludhiana, about 37,000 km², the check still stops: the satellites cannot separate an area that small from the land around it.

Question 9 · the same playbook, somewhere else

Is it the same in Kansas?

Same six steps, different label. Over the US High Plains, a box centred on western Kansas, a straight line falls about 1.3 cm a year (range 0.9 to 1.7). But the drift test does not fire (p = 0.06), so the tool reports the slope as a description and says a record that simply wanders could look like this.

The difference is in the year-to-year changes. In north-west India the average fall (3.7 cm) is nearly as large as the usual swing (4.4 cm). Over the High Plains the average fall (1.5 cm) is about half the usual swing (2.8 cm). Once the test widens its error bar to allow for wandering, that is not enough.

The second method does fire here (p = 0.002). A tool that relied on that test alone would have called the High Plains decline significant. Because that test fired on 21 to 26% of wandering records with no trend, it does not get to decide. The strictness has a price: the tool will sometimes hold back on a decline that is real. What would settle it, in the tool's words: An independent record for the same region, such as observation wells.

Rain matters more here: each extra 100 mm of yearly rain goes with about 2.2 cm more storage (range 0.7 to 3.7). Greenness shows no trend.

How this compares with the 2009 study. Over that study's window, August 2002 to October 2008, this estimate falls 5.3 cm a year. The study reported 4.0 ± 1.0 cm a year for Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana. The outlines differ (a latitude and longitude box here, state borders there), the data releases differ, and this estimate leaves surface water in.

Where the numbers come from. Every figure about the two regions was written out by the TimeDetective code and filled into this page by script. To reproduce it, run scripts/story/extract.py and then scripts/story/build_page.py in the project repository. The false-alarm rates are from the project's calibration notes.
