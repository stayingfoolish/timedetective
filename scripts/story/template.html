Question 1 · step 1

How can anyone know what is underground?

By weighing it from orbit. Two satellite missions, GRACE and then GRACE-FO, run by NASA with German partners, measure tiny changes in Earth's gravity as water moves around. That gives the change in all the water stored in a region: soil moisture, snow, rivers and groundwater together.

A NASA land model, GLDAS, estimates the soil moisture and snow. Subtract that from the total, and what is left is an estimate of groundwater. Everything is counted in centimetres of water: a layer that deep spread over the whole region.

Question 3 · step 3

Is that real, or could it be noise?

It is larger than chance would explain. This is the question the challenge is about, and a fitted line cannot answer it, because a line can be fitted to anything.

Records like this one wander. A wet year leaves the ground wetter the next year too, so a record with no trend at all can drift one way for a decade and look like a decline. The test used here is built for that. It ignores the line, takes the change from each year to the next, and asks whether those changes average out to zero.

They do not. The average is a fall of {{mean_change}} cm a year, nearly as large as the usual swing from one year to the next ({{sd}} cm), and it kept up across {{n_changes}} changes. The test's p-value is {{p}}, well under its 0.05 bar.

Step 3 also records what the label will depend on. The smallest steady trend this record could detect is about {{mdt}} cm a year, and the slope is roughly {{times_mdt}} times that. And the direction holds when the first or last few years are dropped: the slope stays between {{win}} cm a year.

Question 4 · step 4

Could it be the satellites and not the water?

The check finds no clear sign of it, though this is the closest call on the page. The record comes from two missions with {{gap_months}} months between them. If the second read a little lower than the first, that step alone would look like a decline.

So step 4 measures the change across the gap after taking out the overall slide, then makes the same measurement across every other stretch of the same length in the record.

Across the real gap, the estimate dropped {{obs}} cm more than the slide alone predicts. A typical stretch moves about {{typical}} cm. {{as_large}} of the {{n_plac}} other stretches, {{share}}%, moved as much or more. That is on the large side, but the bar for raising a caution is 1 in 20, and this does not pass it.

Question 5 · step 5

Is it just a dry spell?

No. Rainfall here rose over the period, and the water still fell.

Groundwater rises after wet years and falls after dry ones, so a run of dry years could explain a decline. Step 5 sets each year's change in storage against the rain of the same two years, the comparison the 2009 study of this region made.

In a spell of exactly average rainfall, {{mean_rain}} mm a year, storage still fell about {{at_avg}} cm a year (range {{at_avg_range}}). Rainfall itself rose by about {{rain_up}} mm a year each year over {{rain_y0}} to {{rain_y1}} (range {{rain_up_range}}).

Question 6 · step 6

Would you see it from the fields?

No. Over {{green_y0}} to {{green_y1}} the land got greener, by about {{green}} NDVI a decade (range {{green_range}}). Looking at the fields alone, nothing would suggest the water under them was falling.

NDVI is a satellite measure of how much green, growing plant cover there is. It comes from NASA's MODIS instrument and is averaged here over the same blocks.

What you hold at this point is the evidence the scientists used: the water fell, the rain did not, and the crops kept growing. The 2009 study in Nature by Rodell, Velicogna and Famiglietti concluded that pumping for irrigation was the likely cause. That is their conclusion, drawn with knowledge of how the region farms. Testing it needs pumping records, and no step in this playbook has any. A tool that printed “caused by irrigation” here would be repeating a paper, not checking one.

Question 9 · the same playbook, somewhere else

Is it the same in Kansas?

Same six steps, different label. Over the US High Plains, a box centred on western Kansas, a straight line falls about {{hp_slope}} cm a year (range {{hp_range}}). But the drift test does not fire (p = {{hp_p}}), so the tool reports the slope as a description and says a record that simply wanders could look like this.

The difference is in the year-to-year changes. In north-west India the average fall ({{mean_change}} cm) is nearly as large as the usual swing ({{sd}} cm). Over the High Plains the average fall ({{hp_mean}} cm) is about half the usual swing ({{hp_sd}} cm). Once the test widens its error bar to allow for wandering, that is not enough.

The second method does fire here (p = {{hp_p2}}). A tool that relied on that test alone would have called the High Plains decline significant. Because that test fired on 21 to 26% of wandering records with no trend, it does not get to decide. The strictness has a price: the tool will sometimes hold back on a decline that is real. What would settle it, in the tool's words: {{hp_strengthen}}

Rain matters more here: each extra 100 mm of yearly rain goes with about {{hp_resp}} cm more storage (range {{hp_resp_range}}). Greenness shows no trend.

Behind the answers

How the label is decided

Procedures never choose the label. They record evidence. One module, the critic, reads that evidence and applies a fixed rule.

{{checks}}
robust

Everything below, plus the direction holds when years are dropped, a second method agrees, and an independent record agrees. Nothing in this project reaches this rung yet, because no well record has been compared.
